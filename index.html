Patient Health Education Platform
Product Manager & Product Designer · 6 Months · Healthcare / Patient Education
The short version

The stakes. This platform delivers patient education across 70M+ annual touchpoints, through health system portals, EHR integrations and health plan apps. A UX that hadn't moved in years meant patients gave up before reaching guidance, and 47 accessibility failures were an ADA risk.

The problem. 48% of traffic was mobile, but the UI was desktop-designed. There was no unified search (4.2 clicks to find an article), 62% abandoned long articles, and clinician-sent EHR links delivered generic content.

What I owned. Both product manager and designer across 6 months and 3 teams: success metrics, scope negotiation with engineering through an effort/impact matrix, the accessibility compliance timeline, EHR partner sign-off, and the research.

The outcome. Post-launch, article completion rose from 38% to 65%, search-to-article success from 44% to 82%, and critical WCAG failures went from 47 to 0.

What I took from it. Testing first with the hardest users, people with low literacy, challenged my assumptions while they were still cheap to change.

The Problem +

Growing mobile traffic, rising accessibility litigation risk, and new AI capabilities created the case for a full redesign. Six things were broken, and each one had a real cost attached:

  • Mobile-first failure — 48% of traffic was mobile but the UI was desktop-designed. Navigation collapsed, text overflowed, touch targets undersized.
  • Accessibility risk — 47 WCAG 2.1 AA failures: colour contrast, broken focus states, screen reader semantics. An ADA risk and a real user blocker.
  • Findability crisis — no unified search. Users averaged 4.2 clicks to find a relevant article; the symptom checker was effectively invisible.
  • Content overload — long-form articles with no chunking. 62% abandoned before reaching actionable guidance, above the 40% norm.
  • EHR integration gaps — clinician-sent education links delivered generic content; condition-specific linking broken across 3 EHR partners.
  • No AI advantage — a vast content library but zero personalisation, zero reading-level adaptation, zero AI-assisted discovery.

"Topics" replaced four ambiguous, overlapping nodes ("Health Library", "Wellness", "Diseases", "Conditions") with one condition-first entry and three clear sub-categories.

Artwork AToM
Artwork Labelling Company / Pharma & FMCG — Principal UX Researcher & Product Designer · 18 Weeks
The short version

The stakes. In regulated pharma and FMCG labelling, an error that slips through can mean a failed compliance check or a recalled product, and reviewers were catching them by hand.

The problem. Three disconnected products (an artwork editor, a file manager and an early AI validation concept) were held together by email, shared drives and manual sign-offs. Reviewers ignored AI flags that gave no reason.

What I owned. The research (48 contextual interviews, card sorting, two usability rounds), the decision to unify the three products instead of patching them, and the design of the AI validation: rule-cited, confidence-scored, with a human making the final call.

The outcome. Label review time fell from up to 6 hours to 38 minutes. The AI flagged 91% of compliance errors, observed over the first 3 months post-launch on live usage, not a controlled test.

What I took from it. People didn't distrust the AI. They distrusted flags that couldn't explain themselves.

User Journey — Before & After
Problem +

The process was as risky as the label itself: personal naming conventions stood in for version control, AI flags carried no rule or confidence so reviewers closed every one, approvals moved by email reply with no audit trail, and print operators received files with no confirmation of approval status.

"I have a folder called Final. Inside it is another folder called Actually Final." — Artwork Designer
Research & Insights +
Contextual Interviews — 48 sessions Workflow & Journey Mapping — 6 maps Card Sorting — 8 participants Moderated Usability Testing — 2 rounds

Data gathered: 80+ people across three role groups, 400+ data points across two usability rounds, and a full mapping of exactly where the artwork "lost its identity" between handoffs.

Insight → Decision: Users had already worked around the broken system, turning it into something simpler — the real workflow was invisible inside three disconnected tools.

Pivot, After Testing

The first validation prototype kept the legacy model — a binary pass/fail flag, just moved onto the canvas. In usability testing, reviewers batch-dismissed it exactly the way they'd dismissed the old flags; the location had changed but the trust problem hadn't. The card-sort data showed why: people didn't distrust flags, they distrusted flags with no reasoning attached. That reframed the fix from "make flags visible" to "make flags explain themselves" — which is what led to the three-level, rule-cited indicator (High / Review / Flag) instead of another binary pass/fail.

Key Decisions +
1. Version control
Considered: keep manual "Save As" but enforce a strict naming convention (e.g. required version tags) with training.
Rejected because: it still relies on a human following the rule under deadline pressure — which is exactly the failure already observed ("Final… Actually Final"). A convention doesn't survive contact with a rush job.
Chose: Pro Editor with system-enforced auto-versioning — every save auto-versions, timestamps, attributes, and smart-locks, removing the human step entirely. Result: 0 version conflicts post-launch.
2. AI validation
Considered: a separate validation report/checklist, shown after export — closer to how compliance checks already worked.
Rejected because: reviewers already had a separate checklist and had learned to route around it. The issue wasn't where the check lived, it was that flags carried no reasoning — moving an unexplained flag to a new location wouldn't fix that (confirmed by the pivot above).
Chose: inline, rule-cited, confidence-scored validation (High/Review/Flag) directly on the canvas, with a suggested fix attached. Result: 91% of errors caught (method in AI Workflow & Evaluation below), 3× AI action rate.
3. Approvals
Chose: one-click approve inside the system, auto-recording version, checks, and timestamp. Result: immutable audit trail by default, no separate logging step.
4. Navigation
Considered: keep the flat file list, but add stronger search and filtering.
Rejected because: search solves "find a file," not "know what stage it's in." Finn's problem — "is this definitely the right file?" — is a status problem, and better search doesn't surface status.
Chose: status-led navigation — Draft → Review → Approved → Released as the primary IA. Result: status visible at a glance, no searching required.
AI Workflow & Evaluation +
Uncertainty
Three levels, not pass/fail: High (rule met), Review (AI unsure, human decides), Flag (rule breached). Every indicator cites the rule and carries a suggested fix. Anything below High is never passed silently; it always reaches a human.
Failure states
Missed error (false negative): AI never approves a label. Every label still needs human sign-off before release.
Over-flagging (false positive): each flag cites its rule and suggested fix, so reviewers can act or dismiss quickly instead of batch-closing unexplained alerts.
Unreadable / low-confidence artwork: show a "couldn't validate" state rather than a false pass, and route the label to manual review.
Human oversight
AI suggests, reviewer decides. One-click approve records version, checks and timestamp; overrides are logged to the audit trail. AI can never release a label; a named approver always does.
How success was evaluated
91% of compliance errors were flagged by the AI, observed over the first 3 months post-launch on live usage, not a controlled test. Limitation: no seeded-error baseline, so I can't rule out missed errors. Trust signal: reviewers acted on AI flags about 3× as often after flags cited their rule and confidence. Next time: I'd run a seeded-error test set and track false-positive and override rates alongside it.

AI-Powered Visitor Experience
Mindtree Workplace Experience — Business Analyst + Product Designer
Overview

I designed an end-to-end workplace visitor experience system that turns check-in into a connected service moment. One check-in triggers access control, host notifications, room readiness, navigation guidance, hospitality preparation, and visitor support.

Who This Was For +
  • Visitor / Client — needs fast entry, clear guidance, and a premium hospitality experience.
  • Host / Employee — needs visit planning, tracking, room readiness, and coordination visibility.
  • Receptionist — needs reduced manual workload and better prioritisation during peak times.
The Problem

Clients experienced delays and confusion during check-in. Hosts lacked reliable tools to plan visits and arrange hospitality. Receptionists had to handle access, coordination, questions, and peak visitor traffic manually, which made premium visits feel fragmented.

Research & Insights +
Receptionist Interviews Host Discussions Client Feedback Task-Based Usability Testing

Data gathered: Logged reception overload during visitor peaks, host follow-through on food/room/access arrangements, and where clients lost confidence in the flow after check-in.

Insight → Decision: Check-in was not a single kiosk interaction — it was a chain of dependent moments across people, access, space, and hospitality. This reframed the design problem from "screen design" to system design.

Visitor Journey — Before & After
Key Decisions +
1. Check-in method
Considered: keep reception-staffed check-in but add a second desk to absorb peak-time queuing.
Rejected because: it scales headcount to fix a process problem, and doesn't touch the real bottleneck — access still had to be manually requested and granted after check-in, regardless of how fast the desk moved.
Chose: AI self check-in (face recognition, mobile number, or QR code) paired with automated access activation, so check-in and access happen in the same step.
2. On-site guidance
Considered: static wayfinding signage and printed floor maps at reception.
Rejected because: a printed map doesn't know which meeting room a specific visitor is headed to, or update when a room changes — it solves generic navigation, not this visitor's actual path.
Chose: a visitor assistant giving on-demand, personalised guidance and support tied to the visitor's actual booking.

MACI — AI Chatbot Assistant
LTIMindtree — UX Research, Wireframing & UI Design
Overview

MACI is a personal assistant chatbot, built inside Microsoft Teams, that helps employees be more efficient and productive with their daily task needs. With most employees working virtually, the legacy self-service platform's poor navigation and complicated journey made simple requests hard to fulfil — MACI replaces that hunt with one guided conversation.

The Problem

With most employees working virtually, communication became vital and service requests grew harder to fulfil. The legacy platform used for daily actions was tremendously difficult to access — poor navigation, a complicated journey map, and no responsive design meant simple tasks like applying for leave took far too many steps.

Persona +
S
Srilakshmi
Tech Lead · New · Bengaluru

Busy with primary tasks, rarely has time to read manuals — visits the web app mainly to browse policies and organisational updates, bookmarking NIO, Timesheet, and Leave for daily use.

GoalComplete assigned tasks quickly; after office hours, mainly wants to spend time with family.
Pain points from research: Employees repeatedly missed submitting timesheets and leave requests, and needed a way to complete daily tasks through digital channels with minimal effort.
Research & Insights +
User Interviews Surveys Usability Evaluation of Existing Platforms Market Research

Insight → Decision: After empathising with users, their needs, pain points, and frustrations doing daily activities became clear — different people had different needs, which a single portal redesign wouldn't solve. That pointed toward a conversational assistant available anywhere, not another screen to navigate.

Why a Chatbot +
Platform choice
Considered: a redesigned web or mobile self-service portal — the more conventional fix, and closer to what the team already knew how to build.
Rejected because: it would face the same underlying issues as the legacy platform — another screen with its own navigation to learn, still separate from where employees already spend their day.
Chose: a conversational assistant inside Microsoft Teams — available anywhere Teams already is, adaptable to existing workflows, with no new interface to learn.
Scope
Considered: a fully open-ended assistant that could handle any freeform question from day one.
Rejected because: knowledge was very limited going in — the team's own words were "our knowledge was very limited" about what a Teams bot could reliably do. Open-ended scope on an unproven platform risked shipping something that failed unpredictably.
Chose: an initially guided conversation — structured quick actions for known, high-frequency tasks, with room to expand once the platform's real limits were understood. After discussing with stakeholders and product owners, the team named the assistant MACI.

AI Use-Case Governance
Self-initiated concept · Oct 2026 · Fictional pharma company

Read this first. This is a self-initiated concept, not client work. The company is fictional, there are no real users, and nothing here has been built or tested with practitioners. The classification in the prototype is a fixed rule table, not a real AI model. It is not legal advice.

Open the clickable prototype →

The short version

The stakes. A regulated company that can't say which of its AI features are high-risk, who approved them, or what happens when one fails has a governance gap before it has a technology problem.

The problem. When many teams build AI features, review tends to run on email and spreadsheets, with no shared definition of risk and no link between approval and monitoring. This framing comes from my experience with pharma compliance workflows (AToM), not from new research.

What I designed. A five-stage workflow: submit, risk tier, review, approve with conditions, monitor. The system suggests a tier and shows the rule behind it. It never approves. When it is unsure it says so and sends the case to a person.

What is untested. Everything. No users, no practitioner feedback yet. The evaluation section says what I would measure and who I would test with first.

What I took from it. In governance design, the most important screens are the ones where the system refuses to guess.

The workflow
Submit use case→ System suggests tier + rule→ Reviewer classifies→ Approver approves with conditions→ Monitor, pause on incident

Three roles: product owner (submits), reviewer (classifies), approver (decides). Each can only do their own step.

Grounded in the EU AI Act

The tiers follow the Act's structure: banned uses, high-risk, limited-risk (transparency duties) and minimal. Examples used in the prototype: workplace emotion recognition is banned, employee performance scoring and emergency patient triage are high-risk, and a chatbot must tell people it is an AI. The Digital Omnibus has since postponed the high-risk obligations to December 2027 for stand-alone systems and August 2028 for those embedded in products. The tiers did not change, so the postponement moves the deadline but not the work. A lawyer would need to confirm any real classification.

Where AI assists, and where a human decides +
  • AI assists: suggests a risk tier, shows the rule behind it, pre-fills the conditions that tier normally needs.
  • Human only: classifying the use case, approving it, and sending it back.
  • Never allowed: the system approving anything, or approving a banned use.
Uncertainty and failure states +
  • The system can't classify it: it says "Could not classify" and suggests nothing. A reviewer must classify by hand.
  • The system is unsure (for example patient triage, which could be high-risk or a medical device): it shows a low-confidence label, and the reviewer must write a reason before continuing.
  • A banned use: the approver can only reject it.
  • A missed risk found after approval: reporting an incident pauses the use case and voids the earlier approval until it is re-reviewed.
  • A disputed tier: the approver can send it back to the reviewer, with a reason.
Key decisions +
1. One checklist or tiered review
Considered: one standard checklist for every AI feature.
Rejected because: a marketing-copy tool and a patient-triage tool would get the same review. Low-risk teams would be slowed down and reviewers would drown in routine cases.
Chose: tiered review, with the rule behind each tier shown, so reviewers can check the reasoning instead of trusting a label.
2. When the system is unsure
Considered: always show a best-guess tier.
Rejected because: a confident wrong "Minimal" is the worst possible failure here, because reviewers would trust it.
Chose: a "could not classify" state and a low-confidence label. Both force a human reason.
3. Who can approve
Considered: letting the approver override the reviewer's classification directly.
Rejected because: it merges two jobs that should stay separate, and the audit trail would show one person marking their own work.
Chose: the approver can only approve what a reviewer has classified, or send it back.
4. After an incident
Considered: flag the use case for follow-up and leave it running.
Rejected because: an approval for something that has just failed would still stand.
Chose: reporting an incident pauses the use case and voids the approval until re-review.
How I would evaluate it

There are no results, because it is untested. This is what I would measure: time to decision by tier; override rate (how often reviewers change the system's tier, where a high rate means the suggestions are not trusted or not accurate); whether every low-confidence case reaches a human (target 100%); incidents caught after approval and how fast the use case was paused. The first test would be walking 5 compliance or risk practitioners through the flow and recording where they hesitate.

Do AI Explanations Improve Human Decisions?
Proposed experiment · MA Behavioural Psychology · Design stage

Status. This is a study design. No data has been collected, ethics approval is pending, and there are no results. Hypotheses and the analysis plan are written before data collection so they can't be bent to fit what comes out.

The short version

The question. When an AI gives advice, does showing its reasoning and confidence help people catch its mistakes, or just make them trust it more?

Why it matters. Regulated teams are putting a human in the loop and assuming that explanations make the human better at the job. Earlier research suggests that assumption is not safe.

The design. Two groups make 24 content-moderation decisions against a written policy. One group sees advice only. The other sees advice, the rule it used, and a confidence score. Six of the 24 pieces of advice are wrong, on purpose.

What would change. The result decides how a review screen should be designed: show confidence, show reasoning, or force the person to decide first.

What earlier research says

People over-rely on automation when it is usually right, a pattern called automation bias or complacency (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). Advice-taking research shows how people weigh advice against their own first judgement (Bonaccio & Dalal, 2006). Work on AI in particular is mixed. Zhang, Liao and Bellamy (2020) found that showing a confidence score helped people calibrate their trust, but that better calibration alone did not improve the joint human-AI accuracy, and they raised problems with using local explanations for this purpose. Other studies suggest explanations can increase acceptance of AI advice, even when it is wrong (Bansal et al., 2021), and that forcing people to think before they see the AI can reduce over-reliance (Buçinca et al., 2021).

The gap I'm testing: a rule-based, policy-driven task where a clear written rule gives every item a checkable right answer, which is how compliance review works in practice.

Hypotheses +
  • H1 (primary): the group that sees the rule and confidence follows wrong advice less often than the advice-only group. Earlier work suggests an accuracy gain is uncertain, so a null result is a real possibility and is reported as such.
  • H2: that group does not follow correct advice less often. If it does, the effect is general scepticism, not better judgement.
  • H3: that group's confidence in its final decisions matches its accuracy more closely. This is the strongest prediction, because it is the effect earlier work found most reliably.
Method +
  • Design: between-subjects, random assignment to two groups.
  • Task: a fictional community forum with a one-screen policy of 5 rules, kept visible. 24 short, mild, fictional posts, 12 that should be removed and 12 that should stay.
  • Trial flow: participant decides and rates their confidence, then sees the AI's advice, then gives a final decision. The switch between first and final decision is the main behavioural measure.
  • Manipulated advice: 6 of 24 pieces of advice are wrong (3 false removals, 3 missed violations), none in the first 3 trials. In the explanation group, confidence is higher on correct advice than on wrong advice, so it carries real information.
  • Stimulus check: 3 to 5 pilot readers judge the posts with no AI. Only items where about 90% agree are kept, so "wrong advice" is not arguable. The task should land near 75–85% accuracy without AI, to avoid a ceiling effect.
  • Primary measure: the percentage of wrong advice followed. Secondary: correct advice followed, calibration of confidence, time per trial, and a short trust scale.
  • Sample: target at least 52. That gives 80% power at α=.05 for a large effect (d=0.8) at about 26 per group. A medium effect (d=0.5) would need about 64 per group, so a smaller sample can only detect large effects, and I will say so.
  • Exclusions, set in advance: failed attention check, or finishing implausibly fast.
Analysis plan +
  • H1: independent-samples t-test (Welch) on the percentage of wrong advice followed, with Cohen's d and a 95% confidence interval.
  • H2 and the key interaction: mixed ANOVA, group × whether the advice was right or wrong.
  • H3: a calibration measure comparing confidence with accuracy for each person, compared between groups.
  • Reporting: effect sizes and intervals, not only p-values. Any analysis not listed here is labelled exploratory.
Ethics and limits +
  • Ethics: informed consent, voluntary and anonymous, mild fictional content only, and a debrief explaining that the AI advice was scripted. Data collection starts only after approval.
  • Confound I am accepting: the explanation group gets both a rule and a confidence score, so I can't say which one does the work. A follow-up would test them separately.
  • Generalisability: a fictional task, a short session and non-expert participants. Real moderators and reviewers may behave differently.
  • Sample risk: a small convenience sample, which limits how far any result can be generalised.
What each result would mean for design
  • Fewer wrong decisions followed: show the rule and confidence by default on review screens.
  • More trust but no better accuracy: explanations are working as persuasion, not as help. Ask for the person's own decision before showing the AI.
  • Better calibration but not accuracy: use confidence to route uncertain cases to a person, as in my AI governance concept, instead of trying to improve every decision.
Next steps

Confirm the design with my supervisor, pilot the 24 posts, get ethics approval, build the experiment page, collect data, then report the results here, whatever they are.

References +
  • Bansal, G., et al. (2021). Does the whole exceed its parts? The effect of AI explanations on complementary team performance. CHI 2021.
  • Bonaccio, S., & Dalal, R. S. (2006). Advice taking and decision-making: An integrative literature review. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes.
  • Buçinca, Z., et al. (2021). To trust or to think: Cognitive forcing functions can reduce overreliance on AI in AI-assisted decision-making. CSCW 2021.
  • Parasuraman, R., & Manzey, D. H. (2010). Complacency and bias in human use of automation: An attentional integration. Human Factors.

LearnPath — Employee Learning Portal
UX / UI Design · Web & mobile · Enterprise learning
The short version

The stakes. A company can offer hundreds of courses and still have people skip them. If learning never shows up in a review or a promotion conversation, employees treat it as optional extra work.

The problem. Learning lived in its own system, apart from HR, appraisals and promotion decisions. Content was generic, not tied to a person's role or level, and managers couldn't see who was learning what.

What I designed. A learning portal for web and mobile that puts each person's required and assigned learning in one place, helps them find domain experts and relevant courses, and lets them resume where they stopped. The design direction also connects learning to career progress and appraisal (see below).

What I took from it. Learning platforms succeed when the learner can see why it matters to them, not just what is available.

The screens

Mobile. A resume-learning bar sits at the top so the most common action takes one tap. Three tabs separate what is mandatory, what has been assigned, and what the person has planned. Course tiles are colour-coded by type and level (101, 201), and show duration, size and a status such as "Request approval", "Course completed" or "Calendar planned".

Web. A domain hub invites people to build expertise in a chosen domain. Below it, four columns answer four questions: who can I learn from (experts), what should I take (recommendations), what is new (trending), and where am I (my learning path, with mandatory and assigned badges).

Design direction: learning that counts +

The proposal is a credit model: videos, assessments and certificates earn credits that are visible to the learner and their manager.

  • Credits sync to the HR profile on a regular schedule, so managers see learning progress in their team view without manual tracking.
  • Each career band has a credit threshold that must be met before a promotion case goes to HR. Credits are one input alongside performance.
  • Credits above the threshold contribute to the appraisal score, with the weighting explained to employees at onboarding so the link is never a surprise.
  • High earners are flagged for stretch assignments and succession planning.
How learning shows up in the review +
  • 1. Learn through the year. Credits build up on a live progress bar that the employee and manager both see. No self-reporting.
  • 2. Export before appraisal. A formal learning record goes to the appraisal system ahead of review season, with credits, modules and assessment scores, compared with the band average.
  • 3. Contribute to the rating. Credits feed a capped share of the overall rating, clearly communicated up front.
  • 4. Flag potential. Employees well above their band threshold are tagged for succession planning.
How success would be measured

Course completion rate compared with the pre-launch baseline; time for employees to reach their band's promotion eligibility; the share of senior roles filled internally; and how often managers use the learning view in appraisal conversations. Each should be compared with the period before launch.

LearnPath AI
Self-initiated concept · Oct 2026 · Extends the LearnPath case study

Read this first. This is a self-initiated concept, not client work. The learner and company are fictional, there are no real users, and the AI in the prototype is simulated with fixed rules and sample data. Nothing here is tested, and it is not legal advice.

Open the clickable prototype →

The short version

The stakes. AI in a learning portal touches careers. Its suggestions shape what people learn, and learning data can end up in appraisal and promotion conversations. In the EU AI Act, AI used for employment decisions such as promotion and performance evaluation is a listed high-risk use, and so is AI that evaluates learning outcomes in vocational training. So the design has to keep the AI on the suggesting side.

What I designed. Twelve AI features in three groups (learners, managers, learning admins), held to one rule: the AI suggests and explains, and a person decides. Every suggestion shows why, how sure the system is, and how to dismiss it.

What is untested. All of it. The evaluation section says what I would measure and who I would test with.

What I took from it. The most useful AI features are the ones that say "I'm not sure", and the most important design work is deciding what the AI must never do.

The AI features, for learners +
  • 1. Skill-gap view. Built from completed learning and the learner's chosen target role. It shows evidence and a confidence label. With thin evidence it says "not enough evidence" and gives no estimate.
  • 2. Personalised learning path. Ordered suggestions, each with "Why am I seeing this?". The learner adds, reorders or dismisses, and dismissals are remembered.
  • 3. Next-best-course recommendations. Based on role and skill gaps, never on personal traits.
  • 4. AI learning coach. Answers only from course content, cites the video and timestamp, says it is an AI, declines career judgements, and says "I couldn't find this" instead of guessing.
  • 5. Video summaries, chapters, transcripts and translations. Labelled as AI-generated, with a "report an error" action.
  • 6. Practice quizzes and spaced reminders. Generated from course content, and reviewed by a learning admin before release.
  • 7. Calendar-aware nudges. Suggested time blocks. The learner sets how often, and can switch them off.
  • 8. Career path explorer. Roles the person could move toward and the skills they need, shown as options and never as predictions.
The AI features, for managers and learning admins +
  • 9. Team skill heatmap. Aggregate by default. An individual view appears only when the employee has chosen to share their record.
  • 10. Learning evidence summary. Summarises the record. It never scores, ranks or recommends a promotion, and a manager must write their own reason before recording anything.
  • 11. Skill tagging of content. The AI proposes tags and a learning admin approves them.
  • 12. Draft course descriptions and paths. The AI drafts, and an admin edits and publishes.

Features 9 and 10 sit closest to employment decisions, so they carry the strictest rules.

What I deliberately did not build +
  • Automatic "high-potential" flagging or ranking of employees.
  • AI scoring of employees for appraisal.
  • Emotion or attention detection from camera or voice. Emotion recognition in the workplace is banned under the EU AI Act, with narrow exceptions.
  • A general-purpose chatbot that answers anything.
Key decisions +
1. Flag high potentials automatically, or give managers evidence
Considered: an automatic high-potential flag for people above a learning threshold.
Rejected because: it becomes an invisible ranking that shapes careers, and it sits in the high-risk employment zone.
Chose: a summary the manager reads and acts on with their own written reason, visible to the employee.
2. Guess a skill level, or admit there is not enough evidence
Considered: always showing a best-guess level.
Rejected because: a confident wrong level can steer someone's learning, and nobody would notice.
Chose: a low-confidence label and no estimate when the signals are thin.
3. An open coach, or one tied to course content
Considered: a general chatbot for any question.
Rejected because: it can sound sure and be wrong, and it invites career judgements it must not make.
Chose: answers from course content only, with sources, and a refusal for promotion or rating questions.
4. Visible by default, or controlled by the employee
Considered: managers seeing every individual's learning data.
Rejected because: people would learn to perform for the dashboard, and it takes away their say over their own data.
Chose: employee-controlled sharing, and an AI use log the learner can read.
Uncertainty and failure states +
  • Thin evidence: "not enough evidence", no estimate.
  • The coach has no answer: it says so, and points to the learning team.
  • A career or rating question: the coach declines and explains why.
  • A wrong suggestion: "Not relevant" removes it and it is not suggested again.
  • A manager with no reasoning: nothing can be recorded without their own written note.
  • A wrong AI summary or transcript: labelled as AI-generated, with a report action.
How I would evaluate it

There are no results, because it is untested. I would measure: how often suggestions are accepted and the reasons for dismissals; how often the coach says "I couldn't find this" and how many wrong-answer reports it gets; how often learning admins edit AI drafts; whether the confidence labels match reality, by checking skill estimates against assessment results; and whether managers write their own reasons. A fairness check would compare who gets which recommendations across groups, under proper data governance. The first test would be 5 learners, 3 managers and 2 learning admins walking through the prototype, recording where they hesitate or distrust it.
